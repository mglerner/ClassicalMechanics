Drifting off a Lagrange point

Start a test body a little way from one of the five points, at rest in the rotating frame, and watch what happens, in the frame that rotates with the primaries and in the inertial frame, side by side. Same orbit, same clock. Units as in the handout's side roads: R = 1 and G(M1 + M2) = 1, so one orbit of the primaries takes 2π time units.

0° is the +x direction. At L4, 60° points straight away from the CM, across the ridge of Ueff; 150° runs along the ridge, i.e. along the orbit.

Why a hilltop can hold you

In the rotating frame the body feels three forces. Gravity and the centrifugal force together are −∇Ueff, and L4 is a maximum of Ueff, a hilltop: that arrow always points downhill, away from L4. But the moment the body moves, the Coriolis force appears, at right angles to the velocity, and bends the path sideways. It never changes the speed (it does no work, so the Jacobi constant stays put), it only turns. If the hill is gentle enough, μ < 0.0385, and the displacement is small, the turning wins and the body circles L4 instead of rolling off. A charged particle in a magnetic field is held the same way: a force that does no work can still keep you from leaving.

What the inertial frame sees

Only gravity. The body is on an ordinary orbit around the center of mass, with nearly the same radius and nearly the same period as the Lagrange point itself, so it stays near it; the loops in the rotating picture are the small difference between those two orbits. Tick "window moves with the point" and the inertial view follows the point around its circle: the same loops reappear there, now made by gravity alone. Coriolis and centrifugal are the bookkeeping that turns "a nearby orbit" into "forces" once you insist the primaries stand still.

Things to try

  • Drag μ past 0.0385 (or pick the handout's 0.1). The hill is now too steep for Coriolis to save you.
  • Pick L1 or L2. They are saddles, unstable for every μ: the body is gone within a fraction of an orbit, toward M2 or away from it depending on which way you displaced it. L3 is a saddle too, and goes the same way within an orbit.
  • Change the direction. At L4, 150° is along the ridge: the body is on the same circular orbit, just a little ahead, and barely moves. 60° is across the ridge: now it is on an orbit of a different radius, so a different period, and it drifts a long way along the ring before gravity turns it around. For the Earth and the Moon that loop is about 45 times the displacement; untick the moving window to see it.
  • Sun + Jupiter, L4: this is where the Trojan asteroids live, on exactly those long loops (tadpole orbits). One trip around takes about twelve Jupiter orbits, 150 years; raise the speed.
  • Push the displacement up. Stability is a statement about small displacements: once the loop reaches the primaries' neighbourhood the body leaves, stable point or not. For the Earth and the Moon, 0.05 R at 0° is already too far.
