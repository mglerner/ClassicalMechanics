Why regroup (3.24)?

PHY 317, Chapter 3. The internal-torque step of the proof on Taylor pp. 93-95.

The rule: Newton's third law is a statement about pairs. Any sum you intend to hit with the third law has to be organized by pairs first. The double sum in (3.24) is organized by particles: for each α, all the forces on it. The reaction to Fαβ lives under a different α. Regrouping puts each action and its reaction into the same term, where the third law can act on them.

1. The grid: every ordered pair, folded into unordered pairs

Row α, column β. The cell (α, β) is the term rα × Fαβ. The diagonal is crossed out because a particle exerts no force on itself. The double sum visits every remaining cell. Cells with the same color are the same pair of particles. Hover a cell to see its partner; fold the grid to see the pairs meet.

3. Why each pair then vanishes

After the third law, each pair's torque is (rα − rβ) × Fαβ. The vector rα − rβ points along the line joining the two particles. A central force points along that same line. Two parallel vectors have zero cross product, so each pair contributes nothing, and only the external torques survive: that is (3.26).

Equal and opposite forces alone are not enough. If Fαβ and Fβα were equal and opposite but not along the line between the particles, they would form a couple, and the pair would have a net torque. The regrouping uses the third law; the vanishing uses the central-force assumption. They are two separate steps.
